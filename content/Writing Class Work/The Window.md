In an old house down a limestone road, there was a window that lived in the attic. You could not see this window from the outside; all you saw was a metal roof, but if you pulled down the ladder and walked in, it rested there. If you spent as much time looking through the window as I have, you could hear it yawn as the temperature from midday to the evening fluctuated.
It was intoxicating.
Though I wanted to keep the window to myself, I also had to eat. I would show my friends and tell them to tell people they knew. Before long, people wanted to come see the window. I gathered old couches and chairs along the side of the road and built a theater. I always save a chair for myself.
Ten dollars an hour was a reasonable price to charge for what you saw. There were people like Mrs. Vargas, who would hand me the same hundred-dollar bill every time she came, and I would sneak it back into her purse every day. If anyone deserved to view the window, it was she. The woman raised me and her son when we were teens. She still let me come over for meals after Dennis died. I know she saw him in that window. She had the same unblinking red eyes as she did during his open casket.
A few times word traveled past the county. I knew some of the people that I didn't recognize that came to look through it were part of the government. The window showed me this. It also showed them coming in the middle of the night to attempt to move it and the window refusing to leave. Now though the window shows me a fence around my house. Signs marking the area as a sinkhole while men and women in suits look into my window frantically writing in notebooks. It also shows me how to prevent this.
